\title{What Does a Mixed-Rank LoRA Router Learn?\\
\large Accuracy equivalent to plain LoRA, reproducible routing, one dominant expert}
\author{Author names withheld}
\date{Draft, October 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Mixture-of-experts variants of LoRA promise to adapt capacity to the input: a learned gate
mixes several low-rank adapters per token and per layer. We test a mixed-rank version (experts of
rank 8, 16 and 32, dense per-token gating in every layer) on Qwen2.5-0.5B fine-tuned jointly on
eight tasks, against plain LoRA of matched and smaller size, with five seeds per arm, a learning
rate chosen separately for each arm and a test protocol fixed before the runs.
\textbf{Efficiency:} the gated model is statistically equivalent to every plain LoRA we tried and
better than none. Its macro test loss is $0.7850$ against $0.7836$--$0.7846$ for plain LoRA of
rank 16 to 66; all four differences lie within a pre-specified margin of $0.005$ (two one-sided
tests, Holm-adjusted $p=0.001$) and none is significant. A second round, specified in advance,
goes down to a 2/4/8 mixture and plain ranks 2 to 14 with the same outcome: the mixture is
equivalent to each of them, and rank 2 (1.1M parameters) is within $0.0010$ of rank 16, so
adapter capacity is never the bottleneck here.
\textbf{Interpretability:} trained routing is reproducible across seeds (correlation $0.71$
between seeds' task $\times$ layer $\times$ expert maps, against $0.46$ with equal-rank experts
and $0.02$ for frozen random gates), but most of what makes it look meaningful is not learned.
Frozen random gates are already task-dependent (the task explains 52--63\% of their variance,
against 65--85\% after training), and the trained router sends 71\% of its weight to the rank-32
expert. Removing both small experts from every layer changes the test loss by $+0.0002$. With
ranks 2/4/8 the weight is spread more evenly (45\% on the largest expert) and ordered by task
(tasks with a lower loss use larger experts, Spearman $-0.90$ to $-0.95$ in five seeds), but the
knockouts are unchanged: the largest expert carries the adaptation, and removing the rank-2
expert, which holds a third of the gate weight, changes the mean loss by $-0.0005$. Gate weights
are a reproducible and partly faithful readout of where the dominant expert is used; they are
not a measure of how much each expert matters. Knockouts chosen per task on validation data
lower the gated models' test loss by about $0.002$ in every seed; plain LoRA gets at most a
third of that from the same procedure.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Low-rank adaptation (LoRA) \citep{hu2022lora} fine-tunes a frozen language model by adding a
trainable low-rank update to its weight matrices. Its rank is a single global budget, while
tasks, tokens and layers plausibly need different amounts of adaptation. Mixture-of-experts
routing \citep{shazeer2017moe,fedus2022switch} and adaptive rank allocation
\citep{zhang2023adalora} suggest a natural extension: give each layer several LoRA experts of
\emph{different} ranks and let a learned gate decide, per token, how much of each to use. Similar
LoRA mixtures have been proposed \citep{dou2023loramoe,wu2024mole}; the mixed-rank variant adds an
appealing interpretability hook, because the gate weight on each expert can be read as the
\emph{adaptation capacity} the model chooses to spend.

We ask two questions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Q1 (efficiency).} Is a gated mixture of rank-8/16/32 experts as good as a single
        LoRA with the same number of trainable parameters, or better?
  \item \textbf{Q2 (interpretability).} Does the routing mean something: does it depend on the
        task and the layer, is it reproducible, does it follow task difficulty (harder tasks to
        bigger experts), and is it faithful to what each expert actually contributes?
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Findings.} (i) The gated model is equivalent to plain LoRA of rank 16, 32, 56 and 66
within $0.005$ of macro test loss and better than none of them (Section~\ref{sec:q1}). (ii) Its
routing is reproducible across seeds, which random gates and, to a lesser degree, equal-rank
experts are not; but its task dependence is mostly inherited from the hidden states, it
concentrates on the largest expert, it does not follow difficulty, and it predicts an expert's
importance only for that largest expert (Section~\ref{sec:q2}). (iii) Removing experts per task
gives the gated model a small, consistent test gain, several times what plain LoRA gets from the
same procedure (Section~\ref{sec:pertask}). (iv) A second round with experts of rank 2/4/8 and plain
ranks 2 to 14, specified before it was run, repeats (i) and shows that rank is not binding even
at rank 2. Its routing is more spread and more clearly ordered by task, yet the largest expert
still does almost all the measurable work (Section~\ref{sec:small}).

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
\paragraph{Mixed-rank gated LoRA.} For every transformer layer $\ell$ and every target linear map
$W$ of that layer, we attach $E=3$ LoRA experts $(A_e, B_e)$ of ranks $r_e \in \{8, 16, 32\}$
with $\alpha_e = 2 r_e$. A per-layer gating network (two-layer MLP, hidden size 256, GELU,
dropout 0.1) reads the layer's normalized input $h_t$ for token $t$ and outputs a dense softmax
$g_{t,\ell} \in \Delta^{E-1}$ (no top-$k$). The adapted output is
\begin{equation}
  y_t = W x_t + E \sum_{e=1}^{E} g_{t,\ell,e}\, \frac{\alpha_e}{r_e}\, B_e A_e x_t ,
\end{equation}
where the factor $E$ makes uniform routing equivalent to a plain LoRA of rank $\sum_e r_e = 56$.
The gate is shared by the seven target maps of the layer (attention $q,k,v,o$ and MLP
gate/up/down projections). Two auxiliary losses are added, both warmed up over the first 10\% of
training: the Switch load-balancing loss \citep{fedus2022switch} (weight $10^{-3}$) and the mean
gate entropy (weight $10^{-2}$).

\paragraph{Arms.} Besides the gated model we train: \textbf{LoRA r66}, plain LoRA matched in
trainable parameters (36.29M vs.\ 36.32M for the gated model, gates included); \textbf{LoRA r56},
plain LoRA with rank $8{+}16{+}32$, which is exactly the gated model with routing switched off;
\textbf{LoRA r32} and \textbf{LoRA r16}, smaller budgets; and two controls: \textbf{equal ranks}
(the gated model with experts of rank 19/19/18) and \textbf{frozen random gate} (the gated model
with gates never trained and no auxiliary gate loss, rescaled per layer at initialization so that
their mean top-1 weight matches the trained gates': target $0.723$, achieved $0.725$; the trained
gates' five-seed mean is $0.717$).

\paragraph{Small-rank arms.} A second round (Section~\ref{sec:small}) uses the same gated model
with experts of rank 2/4/8 ($\alpha_e = 2 r_e$, gate networks unchanged; 13.2M trainable
parameters, of which 5.5M are gates) and plain \textbf{LoRA r14} (the 2/4/8 model with routing
switched off, 7.7M), \textbf{r8} (4.4M), \textbf{r4} (2.2M) and \textbf{r2} (1.1M).

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}
\paragraph{Model and data.} Qwen2.5-0.5B \citep{qwen2025qwen25} (24 layers), frozen, fine-tuned
jointly on eight tasks: SQuAD \citep{rajpurkar2016squad}, IMDB \citep{maas2011imdb}, CoNLL-2003
\citep{tjong2003conll}, WikiText \citep{merity2016wikitext}, GSM8K \citep{cobbe2021gsm8k}, XSum
\citep{narayan2018xsum}, CommonsenseQA \citep{talmor2019commonsenseqa} and MNLI
\citep{williams2018mnli}, sampled with fixed weights (0.10--0.15 per task) from at most 20k
training examples per task. Every task is cast as prompt $\rightarrow$ answer text and the loss is
computed on answer tokens only.

\paragraph{Splits.} \emph{Test} is the official evaluation split of each dataset (1000 examples
per task). \emph{Validation} (up to 500 examples per task) is the official validation split
for WikiText, CoNLL-2003 and XSum, whose official test split is labelled, and a held-out part of
the official training split for the other five tasks, cut by article for SQuAD and by premise
for MNLI. An earlier round of these experiments
cut the validation set row by row from the training split; for WikiText every validation
paragraph then came from an article that also had training paragraphs, validation rewarded
memorizing training documents and ranked the arms differently from test. We discarded that round
for model selection, fixed the splits and wrote down the protocol below before rerunning
everything; none of its numbers are used here.

\paragraph{Training.} 5000 optimizer steps of 32 sequences (max length 512), AdamW, cosine
schedule with 10\% warmup, fp16 mixed precision with fp32 adapters, identical for every arm.
The learning rate is chosen per arm as the best validation loss of one seed over nine values
from $10^{-6}$ to $10^{-3}$ (Appendix~\ref{app:lr}): $2.5\times10^{-5}$ for the gated model and
LoRA r66, $5\times10^{-5}$ for r32 and $10^{-4}$ for r16. LoRA r56 and the two controls use the
gated model's rate.

\paragraph{Evaluation and tests.} The main metric is the mean over the eight tasks of the
per-token answer loss (macro answer loss) on the test split at the last training step. For Q1 we
compare the gated model with each plain LoRA over five seeds per arm with a two-sided Welch
$t$-test and, because a non-significant difference is not evidence of equality, with two
one-sided tests \citep{schuirmann1987tost} against an equivalence margin of $0.005$ (about
0.25\% of the gain from fine-tuning, which lowers the loss from $2.80$ to $0.78$). Both families
of four tests are Holm-corrected \citep{holm1979}. The metric, the Welch tests and the Q2 analyses were
fixed before any run of this round; the equivalence margin and three corrections to the routing
analyses were added while the multi-seed runs were training, before any of their results were
read (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}). As a robustness check we repeat the comparison with each task's
loss divided by the base model's loss on that task. Generation is evaluated with greedy decoding:
GSM8K final-number accuracy and XSum ROUGE-L (500 examples each). Controls have three seeds.

Tables~\ref{tab:q1} and~\ref{tab:q1tests} and Figure~\ref{fig:q1} give the main comparison.
\textbf{The gated model is equivalent to each plain LoRA within the margin}: every 95\% interval
of the difference lies inside $\pm 0.0032$ and all four equivalence tests reject a difference of
$0.005$ or more. \textbf{It is better than none}: its mean test loss is the highest of the five
arms, by $0.0004$ to $0.0013$, and no difference is significant. The check with losses divided by
the base model's loss agrees (differences $+0.0003$ to $+0.0010$, Holm-adjusted $p \geq 0.36$),
and so do the test loss at the best-validation checkpoint and the generation metrics, which we
do not test (GSM8K accuracy ranges from $0.345$ to $0.374$ across arms, with a seed s.d.\ of up
to $0.019$).

\textbf{Capacity is not the bottleneck.} LoRA r16, with 8.8M trainable parameters, matches LoRA
r66 with 36.3M ($0.7846$ vs.\ $0.7843$) once each has its own learning rate. Test loss is flat
from rank 16 to rank 66, so a mechanism that reallocates capacity has nothing to gain on this
benchmark, and the result should not be read as evidence for or against gating in a regime
where rank is binding.

\paragraph{Per task.} The per-task losses (Appendix~\ref{app:pertask}) show the same picture.
Against LoRA r66 the gated model differs by at most $0.0033$ on any task; the only differences
well outside seed noise are WikiText ($+0.0033$) and GSM8K ($+0.0024$), both against the gated
model (uncorrected). The frozen random gate is the only arm that stands slightly apart
($0.7864$, three seeds).

\section{Q2: What the router learns}
\label{sec:q2}
All routing statistics average the gate weights over the positions whose next token is an answer
token, on the test split.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig2_routing.pdf}
\caption{Share of gate weight per task (rows) and layer (columns) for each expert, at the
positions predicting answer tokens of the test split, averaged over five seeds.}
\label{fig:routing}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{The router concentrates on the largest expert.} Averaged over tasks and layers, the
rank-32 expert receives $0.71$ of the gate weight, the rank-16 expert $0.18$ and the rank-8
expert $0.11$ (Figure~\ref{fig:routing}). The rank-32 share is $0.69$ in layers 0--7, $0.58$ in
layers 8--15, where the rank-16 expert reaches $0.28$, and $0.85$ in layers 16--23. In those late
layers it ranges from $0.63$ for XSum to $0.96$ for CoNLL-2003; XSum is the only task that keeps
a large share ($0.37$) on the two small experts.

\paragraph{Task dependence is mostly not learned.} The task explains 65\%, 79\% and 85\% of the
variance of the gate vector in early, middle and late layers (a permutation-invariant $\eta^2$).
But frozen random gates, which read the same hidden states through an untrained network, already
reach 52\%, 63\% and 61\%, and equal-rank experts reach 61\%, 79\% and 79\%. Hidden states differ
by task, so any function of them does too; training adds 13 to 24 points, mainly in late layers.
A high $\eta^2$ alone is therefore weak evidence that a router has learned a task structure.

\paragraph{Routing is reproducible.} For each pair of seeds we correlate their task $\times$
layer $\times$ expert maps of gate share, after removing each layer's mean over tasks so that a
depth profile shared by all tasks does not count. The mean correlation is $0.71$ for the
mixed-rank model (ten pairs, $0.66$--$0.78$) and $0.02$ for frozen random gates. For equal-rank
experts, which have no identity, we match the experts of each layer between the two seeds by the
permutation that maximizes the correlation, which can only help this control; it reaches $0.46$
($0.40$--$0.49$). Training therefore converges to a seed-independent allocation, and distinct
ranks make it more reproducible than interchangeable experts do. This is the property of the
router that most clearly depends on learning.

\paragraph{Routing does not follow difficulty.} Measuring difficulty by a task's answer loss,
the correlation between difficulty and expected rank across tasks is weakly negative in every
seed (Spearman $-0.07$ to $-0.52$). Within a task, harder examples are routed to slightly
\emph{smaller} experts ($\rho$ between $-0.15$ and $-0.20$). The intuition that the gate spends
more capacity on harder inputs is not supported.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig3_knockout.pdf}
\caption{Change in test answer loss when one expert's gate weight is set to zero in a block of
layers (no renormalization), mean of five seeds. Red: worse; blue: better.}
\label{fig:ko}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Knockouts: one expert does the work.} We remove one expert by setting its gate weight
to zero in a block of eight layers or in all layers, without renormalizing the others
(Figure~\ref{fig:ko}). Removing the rank-32 expert everywhere raises the macro test loss by
$0.70$ (from $0.785$; the model without any adapter is at $2.80$), mostly through the late
layers ($+0.110$, against $+0.030$ and $+0.015$ for early and middle layers). Removing the
rank-8 or the rank-16 expert everywhere changes it by $-0.0006$ and $-0.0005$, and removing both
by $+0.0002$: in the presence of the rank-32 expert the small experts are redundant on average,
although the two experts alone recover about two thirds of the fine-tuning gain. The average
hides small opposite effects per task: without the rank-16 expert XSum loses $0.006$ while GSM8K
and CommonsenseQA gain $0.006$ and $0.005$. With frozen random gates the work is spread over the
experts (removal of rank 32, 16 and 8: $+0.087$, $+0.009$ and $+0.002$).

\paragraph{Faithfulness is weak overall.} If routing is faithful, the tasks that put more weight
on an expert in a block should suffer more when it is removed there. Our pre-specified summary,
the Spearman correlation across tasks between usage and damage, averaged over the nine expert
$\times$ block conditions and the seeds, is $+0.14$ for the trained router and $+0.07$ for
frozen random gates: a small difference. Looking at the experts separately, which we did not
plan in advance, the correlation is $+0.45$ for the rank-32 expert (positive in all fifteen seed
$\times$ block cases, $0.24$--$0.64$) against $+0.11$ for frozen gates ($-0.24$ to $0.67$), and
about zero for the two small experts, whose removal causes no damage to rank. The gate weights
are thus informative about the one expert that matters and uninformative about the other two.

The knockouts suggest that some adaptation is harmful for some tasks. To test this without
selecting on the test set, we choose for each task and seed the removal that minimizes the
\emph{validation} loss, then score it on test (Table~\ref{tab:pertask}). The removal options were
fixed in advance from the earlier round of experiments. For the gated model the test loss drops
from $0.7850$ to $0.7826$, by $0.0017$ to $0.0028$ in each of the five seeds. Two choices carry
the gain and are made in every seed: for GSM8K, removing the rank-32 expert from the middle
layers ($-0.0115$ on that task); for CommonsenseQA, removing both small experts from all layers
($-0.0060$). SQuAD keeps every expert in every seed. The same procedure on plain LoRA, where a
block's whole adapter is removed, finds almost nothing: $-0.0002$ for r66 and $0.0000$ for r56.

The small-rank round repeats the pattern with one difference. The 2/4/8 model gains $0.0020$
($0.0018$ to $0.0023$ per seed); GSM8K removes both small experts from all layers in every seed.
Plain LoRA r14 gains $0.0006$ ($0.0001$ to $0.0012$), nearly all of it on GSM8K, where every seed
removes the adapter from the early or the middle layers ($-0.0040$ on that task). GSM8K is
therefore slightly over-adapted in plain LoRA too, at least at small rank; the expert structure
finds a larger share of that gain and a second one on CommonsenseQA.

Three cautions apply. The gain is small ($0.3\%$ of the loss) and we did not specify a test for
it in advance. The comparison with plain LoRA is not like for like, because the gated model
offers finer options (one expert in one block) than a plain adapter does. And the edited model
uses the task identity at test time, so its $0.7826$ should not be compared with the unedited
arms of Table~\ref{tab:q1}. What the result does show is that the expert structure makes a
trained model editable per task in a way that survives a validation/test split, in both
mixtures.

\section{A smaller mixture: ranks 2/4/8}
\label{sec:small}
Section~\ref{sec:q1} left open whether gating helps when rank is scarce. After reading those
results and before any new run, we fixed a second round: the same model, data, splits, steps and
metric, with a 2/4/8 mixture and plain LoRA of rank 2, 4, 8 and 14; a nine-point learning-rate
grid from $10^{-5}$ to $5\times10^{-3}$ per arm on one seed (Appendix~\ref{app:lr}), then five
seeds per arm; the Q1 tests against the four plain arms; and a criterion for scarcity: a plain
rank counts as \emph{capacity-bound} if its test loss differs from LoRA r16 (Welch,
Holm-adjusted $p<0.05$ over the four ranks) and exceeds it by more than $0.005$. The selected
rates are $10^{-4}$ for the mixture, r14 and r8 and $2.5\times10^{-4}$ for r4 and r2.

\paragraph{Rank is not binding even at rank 2.} No plain rank is capacity-bound
(Table~\ref{tab:small}): LoRA r2 is $0.0010$ above r16 (Holm-adjusted $p=0.88$), r4 $0.0005$
above, r8 and r14 slightly below. The loss does rise below rank 8, by $0.0019$ from r8 to r2 on
test and $0.0023$ on validation, but this stays under half the margin. A 1.1M-parameter adapter
is thus within $0.0013$ of a 36.3M-parameter one (LoRA r66), and the round cannot say anything
about gating under scarcity; we report it as a second regime where capacity is ample.

\paragraph{Q1 again.} The 2/4/8 model is equivalent to each small plain LoRA within $0.005$ and
better than none: its mean loss lies between those of r14 and r8 (slightly lower) and of r4 and
r2 (slightly higher), and every 95\% interval lies inside $\pm 0.0031$. The check on losses divided by the base model's loss agrees
(differences $-0.0003$ to $+0.0008$, Holm-adjusted $p \geq 0.16$). Equal macro losses hide
per-task differences of up to $0.02$ between ranks (Appendix~\ref{app:pertask}): against r14,
LoRA r2 is better on CommonsenseQA ($-0.0160$) and GSM8K ($-0.0092$) and worse on XSum
($+0.0184$) and WikiText ($+0.0075$). Rank and learning rate change together between these arms, so we do not
attribute the pattern to rank.

\paragraph{Routing is more spread and ordered by task.} The rank-8 expert receives $0.45$ of the
gate weight, the rank-4 expert $0.22$ and the rank-2 expert $0.33$
(Figure~\ref{fig:routingsmall}), against $0.71$, $0.18$ and $0.11$ for the large mixture. The
rank-2 expert is the most used in the middle layers ($0.45$) and the rank-8 expert in the late
ones ($0.59$), where its share ranges from $0.22$ for XSum to $0.85$ for CoNLL-2003, about
twice the spread of the rank-32 share in the large mixture ($0.63$ to $0.96$). The task explains 69\%, 73\% and 84\% of the
gate variance in early, middle and late layers. Across tasks, the expected rank falls as the
task's answer loss rises, with a Spearman correlation between $-0.90$ and $-0.95$ in each of the
five seeds (the large mixture gave $-0.07$ to $-0.52$): CoNLL-2003 and IMDB use the largest
expert most, XSum and WikiText least. Within a task the relation stays weak ($-0.10$ to
$-0.20$). With eight tasks, loss level cannot be separated from task type: the weight on the
rank-8 expert correlates as well with the relative loss reduction from fine-tuning ($+0.88$ to
$+0.90$, not planned in advance), so ``more capacity where adaptation changes more'' fits as
well as ``less capacity for harder tasks''.

\paragraph{What reproduces across seeds and what does not.} The seed-to-seed correlation of the
task-centred maps is $0.61$ (ten pairs, $0.54$--$0.71$), lower than the $0.71$ of the large
mixture and far above the $0.02$ of random gates; no frozen or equal-rank control was trained
for this mixture. The shares per task and block of eight layers vary by at most $0.08$ (s.d.)
across seeds. The depth profile reproduces less well. The correlation between seeds of the
layer $\times$ expert profile (shares averaged over tasks) is $0.61$ ($0.48$--$0.75$), against
$0.94$ for the large mixture; taken per expert it is $0.68$ for the rank-8 expert, $0.52$ for
the rank-2 expert and $0.09$ for the rank-4 expert. At single layers the rank-2 share ranges,
for example, from $0.13$ to $0.73$ (layer 9) and from $0.01$ to $0.61$ (layer 4). How the
weight not given to the rank-8 expert is split between the two small ones at a given layer is
thus largely arbitrary, while the shares per task and block are stable.

\paragraph{Knockouts: still one expert.} Table~\ref{tab:kosmall} repeats the knockouts. Removing
the rank-8 expert everywhere costs $0.42$; the two small experts alone keep about four fifths of
the fine-tuning gain. Removing the rank-2 expert everywhere changes the macro loss by $-0.0005$,
although it holds a third of the gate weight and its update is not small: the Frobenius norm of
$B_e A_e$, averaged over layers, is $0.50$, $0.66$ and $0.92$ for the three experts (same
$\alpha_e / r_e$). Neither the gate share nor the size of the update predicts that this expert
can be removed for free. The rank-4 expert matters in the late layers only ($+0.0035$), and
removing both small experts everywhere costs $0.0072$, against $0.0002$ in the large mixture.
As there, the averages hide opposite effects per task: without the rank-2 expert XSum loses
$0.007$ while CommonsenseQA and GSM8K gain $0.007$ and $0.005$; without the rank-4 expert
WikiText loses $0.023$, all of it in the late layers.

\paragraph{Faithfulness.} The pre-specified summary is $+0.24$ ($+0.14$ for the large mixture).
Per expert it is $+0.48$ for the rank-8 expert, positive in all fifteen seed $\times$ block
cases, $+0.24$ with mixed signs for the rank-4 expert, and $+0.01$ for the rank-2 expert, for
which the middle block is negative in every seed (mean $-0.34$): the tasks that put more weight
on the rank-2 expert there are those that do \emph{better} without it. The reading of
Section~\ref{sec:q2} holds: the gate weights describe the use of the largest expert and little
else.

\section{Discussion}
On this benchmark the gated mixed-rank LoRA reaches the accuracy of a plain LoRA, no more and
no less, with two mixtures. The rank curve explains why it cannot do more: test loss varies by
$0.002$ across plain ranks from 2 to 66, so there is no capacity shortage for a router to
manage. The price is the gate network (5.5M parameters, 42\% of the trainable parameters of the
2/4/8 model) and about 30\% more wall-clock time per run in our setup.

What gating buys at that price is a readout that plain LoRA does not have, and the two mixtures
show how far it can be trusted. Supported in both: the weight on the largest expert, per task
and per block of layers, is stable across seeds and tracks the damage of removing that expert;
and the expert structure allows small per-task edits that hold on test data. Not supported in
either: reading a gate weight as importance. In the large mixture the router sends 71\% of the
weight to one expert and the rest goes unnoticed; in the small mixture the rank-2 expert
receives a third of the weight and can be removed at no average cost. One reading fits both
without being tested here: the share of the largest expert acts as a per-task, per-layer
strength of adaptation, and the remaining weight is parked on experts that matter little.

For interpretability, the controls matter more than the headline numbers. Task-dependent gate
weights are not evidence of learned routing, since random gates have them too. Reproducibility
across seeds is: it is absent from random gates and weaker when experts are interchangeable.
Distinct ranks give the experts an identity that training finds again from one seed to the next.
The gate weights predict the effect of removing the largest expert and nothing about the small
ones, so reading them as ``the capacity the model chooses to spend'' overstates what they
measure.

\section{Limitations}
One base model (0.5B parameters) and one task mixture; the conclusions may change for larger
models or when rank is binding, which this benchmark never is down to rank 2.
\todo{replicate on a second model (Pythia-410M or SmolLM-360M).} The small mixture has no
frozen-gate or equal-rank control and trains at four times the learning rate of the large one,
so differences between the two routing maps are not attributable to rank alone. For that
mixture, the per-layer seed comparison, the correlation with the loss reduction, the update
norms and the per-task removal on LoRA r14 were not specified in advance.
Each arm's learning rate comes from a single seed on a nine-point grid; the validation curves are
flat near their minimum (Appendix~\ref{app:lr}), but a different seed could select a neighbouring
value. Controls have three seeds. The equivalence margin and three corrections to the routing
analyses were added after the learning-rate runs and while the multi-seed runs were training,
before any of their results were read (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}). Inference is over training
seeds; the test examples are fixed. The per-expert reading of the knockout correlations and the
per-task removal gain were not tested with pre-specified tests. Knockouts set gate weights to
zero without renormalizing, which also lowers the total adapter scale. The frozen-gate sharpness
target was measured on test inputs (no labels) of one trained seed.

\section{Protocol and amendments}
\label{app:protocol}
Fixed before any run of this round: the splits, the main metric (plain mean over tasks of the
test answer loss at the last step), the per-arm learning-rate selection on validation loss, the
number of seeds per arm, the Welch tests with Holm correction for Q1, and the list of Q2
analyses. Amended after the first three-point learning-rate grid: the grid was widened once
(Appendix~\ref{app:lr}). Amended while the multi-seed runs were training and before any of their
results were read, following a code review: (i) the equivalence test and its margin of $0.005$
were added, because the original protocol could only report ``no detectable difference'';
(ii) routing statistics are averaged over the positions that predict answer tokens instead of
the answer-token positions themselves, and every routing-bearing model was re-evaluated from
its saved weights with that definition; (iii) seed-to-seed reproducibility is computed on
task-centred gate shares with per-layer expert matching for equal-rank experts, instead of the
correlation of expected-rank maps, which a depth profile shared by all tasks inflates and which
is ill-defined for interchangeable experts. Every reloaded model reproduces its logged test loss
to within $10^{-8}$.

\paragraph{Small-rank round.} Fixed after the results of Sections~\ref{sec:q1}--\ref{sec:pertask}
were known and before any of its runs: the five arms, the nine-point grid and the selection rule
(including that a diverged run counts as worst and that the grid is not extended), five seeds
per arm, the capacity-bound criterion against LoRA r16, the Q1 tests against the four small
plain arms with r16 and r32 as untested references, and the same Q2 analyses on the 2/4/8 model
without new controls. The protocol was followed without amendment. One run's final upload of
its result files failed after training and evaluation had finished; the files were uploaded by
hand from the compute node and nothing was recomputed.
